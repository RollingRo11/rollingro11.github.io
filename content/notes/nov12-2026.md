## Aligning this analysis with the study of LLM Beliefs

(re: Parallax), we obviously want to connect this research to developing an understanding of what LLMs “believe” about an environment. I’d say there are two  ways to perceive your environment as a model (apologies for anthropomorphizing LLMs here):
- ⁣*conscious* beliefs: e.g. beliefs you reason about and then take an action as a result of
- ⁣*subconscious beliefs*: e.g. facts/ideas/knowledge that subtly play a part in determining what you say or do, but not necessarily in a way that you can explain or act upon (for models these are trained biases, memorized problems, etc.)

There are also two types of “reasoning” we might be concerned with:

- explicit reasoning: verbalizing the ways you get to an answer
  - surfaced by the CoT?
- implicit reasoning: non-verbal, but important parts of the reasoning chain
  - Maybe surfaced via a workspace lens, etc.

I should add that both explicit and implicit reasonings are *conscious*.

![](/notes/nov12-2026/belief-venn.png)


So what can learning about robustness tell us about how models perceive their environment when solving problems? Naively, it would be nice to be able to say that a model (given some problem) that is generally not-robust on problems tends to make spurious decisions based on *subconscious beliefs* (on that problem). Basically we’d like to separate when the model decides to do something from when the model just so happens to do something.
- I’ve kinda updated in this direction for small models after SPAR, where after reading a lot of OLMo-3.1’s transcripts I realized that training is weird and that sometimes models just learn to spit out things, without any reasoning!
- The point of mech-interp is to find the *implicit reasoning* or the parts of the model **responsible** for any reasoning, but for the purpose of doing **white-box auditing** I assume the goal of *Parallax* is to surface implicit reasoning.

## Other methods to detect memorization

We no longer have a 1hr cap on what we can run, so I think it will be more interesting to do a short literature review of existing work studying memorization, and aim to form a better “mechanistic understanding” of robustness.

The following is a short literature review of some methods people have used to separate memorized and generalized datapoints:

### Understanding Memorization via Loss Curvature [(Merullo et al., 2025)](https://www.goodfire.com/research/understanding-memorization-via-loss-curvature#)

This is an obvious first choice, most of their interesting results are related to arithmetic! 

For a weight matrix $W$, they take the inputs to $W$, and the gradient wrt $W$’s **output**. They then calculate a covariance matrix for each of these terms (see figure for explanation).

![](/notes/nov12-2026/loss-curvature.png)

They then take the top eigenvectors (sorted by $G$’s top eigenvalues) of the $A$ matrix and project the acts of some datapoint $x$ onto them. If the projection is higher for the higher ranked eigenvectors, the datapoint is more general (AUROC not acc).

We previously tried a version that sorted by $A$’s top eigenvalues (which would be equivalent), and it didn’t work very well. I have three reasons it may not have worked.
- We only did this over problem text, rather than over the reasoning chain.
- [I previously showed](https://rkathuria.com/blog/memorization/) that this method really works best when you fit $A$ and $G$ on a task-relevant dataset. I believe these gains would carry over quite a bit to our setup, at least experimentally.
- We should also project activations onto $A$ times $G$ to get an actual read of the curvature rather than just projecting onto the uncentered PCA of activations.

### Shared circuits predict whether LLMs generalize across formats in arithmetic reasoning [(de Varda et al., 2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.04463?utm_source=extension&utm_medium=abstract-page)

Quick example, we take two problems:
- $x: 44 + 22=$
- $x': 44 - 22=$

Calculate:

$$m(z) = \log P(\hat{y} | z) - \log P(\hat{y}' | z)$$

Where $\hat{y}=66$ & $\hat{y}'=22$. A higher $m(z)$ means the model assigns more probability to the answer it gave for the original problem than to the answer it gave for the sign-flipped problem (unimportant rn).

We then take a gradient attribution of $M(z)$ wrt activations. Specifically, we take the difference in activations between $x$ and $x’$, times the gradient:

$$(a_i(x) - a_i(x')) \cdot \nabla_i M$$

(The activation difference between the original and sign-flipped prompts, times how sensitive the preference score is to that neuron).

What they get from this is a numeric circuit $S_m$, which they formed by taking the top 1% of attributed neurons using the above. Barbecue chicken from there.

![](/notes/nov12-2026/numeric-circuit-loading.png)
*Figure 2: The relationship between numeric circuit loading and performance. (A) Correct items show higher loading than incorrect ones. (B) As loading increases, the probability of a correct answer rises. (C) However, high loading also correlates with increased entropy/uncertainty, suggesting that while the circuit is necessary, it is not always sufficient for a correct answer.*

### Datasets
There are also a bunch of relevant datasets that I think will be useful for benchmarking on:

**Grade-school math (GSM8K-based)**
* GSM-IC ([Shi et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00093)): adds irrelevant sentences
* GSM-Plus ([Li et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19255)): many perturbation types
* MathCheck-GSM ([Zhou et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08733)): checklist of problem variants and task types
* VarBench ([Qian et al., 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.946)): swaps variable values
* GSM-Symbolic ([Mirzadeh et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05229)): templates with new numbers and names, plus irrelevant clauses
* ArithmAttack ([Abedin et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08203)): random punctuation noise
* GSM-DC ([Yang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18761)): controlled distractors built from dependency graphs
* GSM-SEM ([Singh et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07053)): changes entities and relationships but keeps the same calculation

**Harder math (MATH, AIME, Putnam)**
* MATH() / functional MATH ([Srivastava et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19450)): problems as code that generates new versions
* MATH-Perturb ([Huang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06453)): simple vs hard perturbations of MATH level 5
* Putnam-AXIOM ([Gulati et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08292)): functional variants of Putnam problems
* PutnamGAP ([Hao et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08833)): renamed and changed-numbers versions of Putnam problems
* Robust Reasoning Benchmark ([arXiv, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08571)): 14 reversible text transforms on AIME 2024
* AIMO Interpretability Challenge ([Štefánik et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13899)): symbolic variants of olympiad problems
